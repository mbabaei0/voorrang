What beats what

When two things at a junction disagree, the higher one wins. Work down this list until something decides.

  1. A traffic controller’s signal

    A police officer, traffic controller or school crossing guard overrides everything below.

    RVV art. 84

  2. Traffic lights

    A working light overrides the priority signs at the same junction.

    RVV art. 64

  3. Signs and road markings

    Priority signs and haaientanden override the general rules.

    RVV art. 63

  4. General rules

    Traffic from the right, turning, trams and special manoeuvres.

    RVV chapter II

Emergency vehicles sit outside this list: let them go in every situation, unless a traffic controller says otherwise.

Two kinds of giving way

Dutch law uses two different terms, and the exam keeps them apart.

Voorrang verlenen

Give priority. You owe it to drivers only, which means every road user except pedestrians. It applies at junctions and with priority signs.

Voor laten gaan

Let go first. You owe it to all traffic, pedestrians included. It applies when you turn, at zebra crossings and during special manoeuvres.

Topic 1Unmarked junctions

  • Where there are no signs, lights or markings, give way to drivers coming from your right. A T-junction counts as a junction too.
  • Cyclists and moped riders are drivers. A bike from your right goes before you.
  • On an unpaved road, you give way to the paved road, whichever side it comes from.
  • A tram always goes first at a junction, from the right or the left.
  • Pedestrians are not drivers, so you owe them no priority here. Watch out for them anyway.

RVV art. 15

Topic 2Turning

  • When you turn, let oncoming traffic on the same road go first.
  • When you turn left, let an oncoming driver who turns right go first.
  • Turning either way, let cyclists and moped riders who go straight on beside or just behind you go first. This holds on a priority road too.
  • Two oncoming left turners pass in front of each other. Neither has priority, so they can go at the same time.
  • A tram does not have to follow this rule, so let a turning tram go.

RVV art. 18

Topic 3Priority signs and haaientanden

  • B1 means you are on a priority road. B3, B4 and B5 give you priority at the next junction.
  • A give-way sign (B6) or haaientanden on the road mean you give way to every driver on the crossing road, from the left too, and to cyclists on its cycle path.
  • A stop sign (B7) means stop at the line, then give way.
  • Between two road users on the same priority road, the turning rules still decide.
  • When the priority road bends at the junction, following it counts as going straight on.

RVV art. 80, appendix 1

Topic 4Roundabouts

  • No law gives traffic on a roundabout priority. Signs do: a give-way sign or haaientanden on your approach mean you give way as you enter.
  • Without those signs, the right-hand rule applies, so traffic entering goes before traffic already on the roundabout.
  • When you leave, let traffic that stays on the roundabout go first, including cyclists on a cycle path around it.
  • Whether cyclists on the roundabout have priority depends only on the signs and markings, so always look for haaientanden on the cycle path.

RVV art. 15, 18 and 80

Topic 5Traffic lights

  • A working light beats the priority signs at the junction.
  • Green means you may go, not that you go before everyone. Turning on green, you still let oncoming and straight-on traffic go first.
  • An arrow light applies only to the direction of the arrow.
  • When the lights are off or flash yellow, the signs and haaientanden decide. Without those, the general rules apply.

RVV art. 64, 68 and 75

Topic 6Trams

  • At a junction the tram goes first, from the right or the left.
  • A turning tram does not have to let straight-on traffic go.
  • Signs and lights still rank higher: a tram facing a give-way sign or a red light gives way too.

RVV art. 15 and 18

Topic 7Emergency vehicles

  • Let a priority vehicle go first, whatever the signs or lights say. Only a traffic controller ranks higher.
  • It may drive through a red light or on the wrong side of the road when its task requires it.

Exam trap: it only counts as a priority vehicle with a blue light and a siren. With blue lights and no siren, the normal rules apply.

RVV art. 29, 50 and 91

Topic 8Exits, pulling away and erven

  • Leaving an exit (uitrit), pulling away, reversing, making a U-turn and merging are special manoeuvres. You let all other traffic go first, pedestrians included.
  • You recognise an exit at a glance: the pavement or cycle path carries on across it, often with kerb ramps. The right-hand rule does not apply there.
  • When one car leaves an exit as another enters it, the CBR expects the one leaving to go first.
  • Inside an erf (sign G5) the normal rules apply, at no more than 15 km/h. Leaving an erf through an exit is a special manoeuvre.

RVV art. 44, 45 and 54

Topic 9Pedestrians and zebra crossings

  • At a zebra crossing, let pedestrians who are crossing, or clearly about to, go first.
  • When you turn, let pedestrians going straight on along the same road go first.
  • Let blind people with a white cane, and anyone who has trouble moving, go first everywhere.
  • If the pedestrians have a red or flashing yellow light at the crossing, they let traffic go instead.

RVV art. 18 and 49

Topic 10Mixed situations and deadlocks

  • Real junctions combine rules. Work down the list at the top of this page, then use the general rules between whoever is left.
  • Sometimes the rules go round in a circle and nobody has priority. One road user waves another through by agreement, and the rules decide the rest.
